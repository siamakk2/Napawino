First Time in Napa? Read This Before You Book

Napa Wino editors · Updated August 2026

Let the sommelier plan it →

Napa rewards planning more than almost any destination in California. The valley is small, the demand is global, and the difference between a magical trip and a frustrating one usually comes down to a handful of decisions made before you arrive.

1. Getting here

Napa sits about 50 miles (80 km) northeast of San Francisco — roughly 90 minutes by car, longer in Bay Area traffic. The usual airports are San Francisco (SFO), Oakland (OAK) and Sacramento (SMF). There is no passenger rail into the valley, so nearly everyone drives, hires a car service, or joins a tour. If you're flying in from overseas, give yourself a recovery day before your first tasting — jet lag and Cabernet are a rough combination.

2. Pick your season deliberately

Harvest — locally called “the crush” — runs August through November and peaks in September and October: the valley smells of fermenting fruit, and everything is alive and everything is booked. February through April is the insider window: golden mustard blooming between the vines, thin crowds, softer hotel rates. Summer is warm and busy. December is quietly lovely. 3. Reservations are the rule, not the exception

Most Napa wineries are appointment-only, especially on weekends. Book tastings at least a week out, and months out for icons like Opus One or Château Montelena. A handful welcome walk-ins daily — V. Sattui in St. Helena, with its picnic grounds and deli, is the classic. Restaurant math is stricter: Yountville's famous tables should be booked when you book flights.

4. Budget for tasting fees

Standard tastings at well-known estates commonly run from around $40–$75 per person, with reserve and seated experiences climbing well past $100 — and the icons higher still. Many wineries waive or credit the fee against bottle purchases; ask when you book. Two or three tastings a day for two people is a real line item — plan it like dining.

5. Two roads, one valley

Highway 29 is the main artery — the famous names, and the famous traffic. The Silverado Trail runs parallel along the eastern side: faster, prettier, and home to Stags Leap District estates and Darioush. Locals take 29 north in the morning and drift home on the Trail. Order your stops in one direction — south to north or north to south — and never zigzag.

6. The driver rule

Three tastings in, nobody should be driving — for safety first, and because Napa County takes enforcement seriously. Rideshares work on the valley floor (coverage thins in the hills), and local private drivers will run your whole day. Build it into the budget from the start. The Wine Train solves the same problem differently — lunch, tastings and zero driving.

One more thing: manage cult-wine expectations

If your dream is tasting at Screaming Eagle or Harlan Estate — those doors don't open to visitors. Allocation mailing lists, with waitlists measured in years, are the only way in. The consolation prize is real: restaurant cellars like PRESS in St. Helena pour deep Napa lists where the legends occasionally surface.
