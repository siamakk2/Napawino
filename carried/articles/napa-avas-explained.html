The 16 Napa Valley AVAs, Explained

Napa Wino editors · Updated August 2026 · Editorially independent

Ask the Napa sommelier →

Napa Valley is not one place. It is sixteen, and the difference between them is the difference between a plush, generous Cabernet and one built like a cathedral. An AVA — American Viticultural Area — is a federally designated growing region defined by geography, soils and climate rather than county lines. To put an AVA name on a label, at least 85% of the grapes must come from inside it. The overarching Napa Valley AVA was established in 1981; sixteen smaller AVAs nest inside it.

The single most useful distinction is valley floor versus mountain. The floor sits on deep alluvial soils and gets marine fog pulled up from San Pablo Bay each night, which slows ripening and keeps acidity in balance. The mountains sit above that fog line on thin volcanic or sedimentary soils, where vines struggle, berries stay small, and skin-to-juice ratios climb — which is why mountain wines taste firmer, darker and more structured, and tend to age longer.

The valley floor

Oakville

The heart of Napa Cabernet. Gravelly alluvial fans on the western side, heavier clay to the east, and a fog line that usually burns off mid-morning. Home to the To Kalon Vineyard, Opus One, Robert Mondavi, Screaming Eagle and Harlan Estate.

Signature: Cabernet Sauvignon — opulent, cassis-driven, famously age-worthy.

Rutherford

Just north of Oakville, and the source of the phrase “Rutherford dust” — a fine, dusty tannin texture locals swear they can taste. Sandy, well-drained soils; long-established estates including Inglenook, Beaulieu Vineyard, Caymus and Grgich Hills.

Signature: structured Cabernet with red-fruit lift and that distinctive dusty grip.

St. Helena

The valley narrows here, so heat gets trapped and reflected off the hillsides — one of the warmest floor AVAs. Historic winery row since 1876: Beringer, Charles Krug, Joseph Phelps, Duckhorn, Corison.

Signature: rich, broad Cabernet and Zinfandel; ripe and generous.

Calistoga

The valley's hot northern end, on volcanic soils, with big diurnal swings — scorching afternoons, sharply cool nights, which preserves acidity. Château Montelena, Castello di Amorosa, Frank Family.

Signature: dark, concentrated Cabernet and Petite Sirah with firm structure.

Stags Leap District

A small, warm pocket on the eastern side beneath the Stags Leap palisades, which radiate stored heat at night. Volcanic and gravelly loam. The 1976 Judgment of Paris red-wine winner came from here.

Signature: Cabernet with velvet-textured tannins — power without hardness.

Yountville

Cooler than Oakville just to its north, because the marine fog lingers later in the morning. Gravelly and sandy soils. Also the culinary capital of the valley.

Signature: Cabernet and Merlot with fresher aromatics and firm acidity.

Oak Knoll District

One of the coolest floor AVAs, at the southern end where bay influence is strongest. Its long, gentle growing season suits a wider spread of grapes than anywhere further north. Trefethen is the anchor.

Signature: unusually versatile — Chardonnay, Riesling and Merlot alongside Cabernet.

Coombsville

Napa's youngest major AVA (2011), in the hills just east of the city of Napa. Cool, volcanic-ash soils, late ripening, and increasingly the value hunter's answer to Oakville pricing.

Signature: fresh, savoury Cabernet with lower alcohol and bright acidity.

The mountains

Howell Mountain

Napa's first sub-AVA (1983), on the eastern side, entirely above 1,400 feet — which is to say, entirely above the fog. Volcanic ash and tufa soils. Full sun all day, cooler temperatures than the floor.

Signature: dense, tannic, famously long-lived Cabernet and Zinfandel.

Spring Mountain District

Western side above St. Helena, sitting on the Mayacamas range at up to 2,600 feet. Forested, steep and cooler, with more rainfall than the floor. Pride Mountain and Smith-Madrone are here.

Signature: structured mountain Cabernet, and some of California's best Riesling.

Diamond Mountain District

Volcanic soils northwest of Calistoga, dark with ash and glassy obsidian fragments that hold heat into the evening. Schramsberg's historic caves sit here.

Signature: powerful, mineral, tightly wound Cabernet that needs cellar time.

Mount Veeder

The largest mountain AVA by area and the most rugged, on marine sedimentary soils — an ancient seabed, not volcanic. Steep slopes, poor soils, tiny yields. Hess Persson is the best-known visit.

Signature: earthy, firm, distinctly non-plush Cabernet with real longevity.

Atlas Peak

Eastern mountains above Stags Leap, at 760–2,600 feet on volcanic rock, cooled by breezes from the bay. Ripens late and slowly.

Signature: high-acid, deeply coloured Cabernet; also strong Italian varieties.

Cool-climate and outlying

Los Carneros

Straddling the Napa–Sonoma line at the valley's southern mouth, directly exposed to San Pablo Bay fog and wind. Shallow clay soils. The one part of Napa where Cabernet is not the point.

Signature: Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and traditional-method sparkling wine.

Chiles Valley District

A high, remote eastern valley around 800–1,200 feet, well east of the main corridor. Cooler, later-ripening, and home to some genuinely old Zinfandel vines.

Signature: old-vine Zinfandel and restrained Cabernet.

Wild Horse Valley

The smallest and least-visited Napa AVA, on the southeastern rim toward Solano County. Cool, windy, rocky volcanic soils, and very few producers.

Signature: cool-climate Chardonnay and Pinot Noir.

How to actually use this

When you're standing in front of a wall of bottles, the AVA on the label is the most reliable shortcut you have to what's inside. Three rules of thumb:

  • Want plush and immediately delicious? Look for Oakville, Rutherford or St. Helena on the label.
  • Want structure that rewards a decade in the cellar? Look for Howell Mountain, Spring Mountain, Diamond Mountain or Mount Veeder.
  • Want freshness, or something that isn't Cabernet at all? Look for Carneros, Coombsville or Oak Knoll.

One planning note that costs people entire afternoons: the mountain AVAs are 20 to 30 minutes of switchbacks each way from the valley floor, with unreliable cell service. Never schedule two different mountains on the same day — pick one, and build the rest of the day on the floor beneath it.
