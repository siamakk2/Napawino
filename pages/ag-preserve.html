Why It Still Looks Like This

Napa Wino editors · Updated September 2026 · Editorially independent

Drive north from Napa and the thing you notice isn't the wineries. It's what isn't there. No strip malls, no tract housing, no petrol station every mile. Thirty miles of farmland with towns at intervals, which is not how land near a major American city normally ends up.

That's not an accident, and it isn't old-fashioned charm. It's a zoning ordinance passed by four county supervisors on a Tuesday in 1968, over furious objection.

What they were afraid of

Santa Clara Valley. Sixty miles south, it had been one of the most productive orchard regions in America — prune, apricot, cherry — and by the mid-1960s it was being paved at speed. It's Silicon Valley now. Napa's supervisors watched it happen in real time.

Napa County is close enough to San Francisco that the same was coming. Landowners in the late 1950s and early 60s could see their land values about to climb, and the way you realise that value is by subdividing.

The vote

On 9 April 1968 the Napa County Board of Supervisors voted 4–0 to create the first agricultural preserve in the United States. The ordinance did something genuinely radical: it declared agriculture the highest and best use of the land, and forbade splitting parcels below 20 acres — later raised to 40.

It was not popular. The valley split publicly. John Daniel of Inglenook opposed it; Louis P. Martini supported it. Property owners sued almost immediately and lost — a visiting judge in Napa County Superior Court ruled for the county.

Napa voters have reaffirmed the commitment since, through Measures J and P, which moved the decision out of the supervisors' hands and into the electorate's.

What it actually protects

Around 32,000 acres, with roughly 31,600 held against urbanisation until 2058. A further 37,100 acres — about 7.3% of the county — sit under permanent conservation agreements with the Land Trust of Napa County.

The number that surprises people: only about 9% of Napa County is planted to vineyard. Around 45,000 acres out of 504,450. Less than 3% of the county remains suitable for planting. The valley reads as wall-to-wall vines because you're driving through the narrow strip where they are.

Nationally the US loses close to 40 acres of farmland an hour to sprawl. Not one acre of Ag Preserve-zoned land has gone to urban use since 1968.

Why a visitor should care

Because it explains the thing you came for. Napa is expensive and crowded partly because of this ordinance — supply of everything is capped by law. There's no new subdivision absorbing demand, so hotel rates and tasting fees go where they go.

It also explains the shape of a day here. Wineries can't become entertainment complexes; the Winery Definition Ordinance that followed limits what a winery may do beyond making wine. That's why Napa tasting rooms feel like working facilities rather than theme parks, and why the ones that push hardest against the line get local pushback.

If you're looking at property

The 40-acre minimum is the fact that matters and the one most buyers hear late. Ag Preserve land cannot be split below it. The 2058 horizon on the protected acreage is the other. Neither is obscure; neither shows up in a listing. Our real estate primer covers what this means in practice.

Seeing it

The Napa Valley Museum in Yountville has an Ag Preserve exhibition, revisited in 2026 to cover what's changed — wildfire and fire-hardening, the regulatory picture, population pressure, and a green certification effort now under way. A genuinely good hour, and almost nobody goes.

Otherwise: drive the Silverado Trail instead of Highway 29, late in the day. Fewer cars, and the valley floor reads as farmland rather than as a wine route.
