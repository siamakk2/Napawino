What to Expect at Your First Napa Wine Tasting

Napa Wino editors · Updated September 2026 · Editorially independent

If you've never done this before, here's the only thing worth knowing going in: nobody at the tasting bar is testing you. The person pouring has served people in flip-flops at a $200-a-bottle estate and people who couldn't name a single grape variety. Their actual job is to help you find something you like. Everything below is just the practical stuff — what happens, what to wear, what to say, and what things cost — so the whole afternoon feels easy instead of nerve-wracking.

"I like this one" and "this is too dry for me" are both completely valid things to say. You don't need better vocabulary than that.
A standard flight: 4-6 pours, about an ounce each Comfortable shoes — gravel paths and cave tunnels are common Weekdays mean smaller crowds and more time with your host

What actually happens

You walk in (or check in for your reservation), pay a tasting fee — commonly $15–75 in Napa depending on the winery — and a host pours you a flight of four to six wines, about an ounce each. You'll typically move through them lightest to fullest, with the host explaining each one and answering whatever you ask. The whole thing runs 45 minutes to a couple of hours depending on the format. That's it. There's no test at the end.

You don't have to finish every glass

Tasting rooms expect you to sample, not drink. Spit buckets sit on every bar for exactly this reason, and everyone from total beginners to professional sommeliers uses them — especially if you're visiting more than one winery that day, which is the whole point of pacing yourself across a Napa afternoon. Pouring out a wine you don't love, or asking to skip one entirely, is completely normal.

What to wear

Comfortable and put-together is the target — jeans and a nice top, a sundress and flats, a button-down with the sleeves rolled up. One notch above what you'd wear to run errands. More important than looking sharp: comfortable shoes. Many tastings involve walking gravel paths, vineyard rows, or cave tunnels, and heels on gravel is a rough afternoon. Skip heavy perfume or cologne — scent genuinely affects how the wine smells, for you and everyone else at the bar.

What to say (you don't need wine vocabulary)

"What am I tasting?" and "What food would this pair with?" are two questions every host is happy to answer at any point. Beyond that, your own honest reaction is the whole exercise — "I really like this one" or "this isn't for me" tells the host exactly what they need to guide you toward something better. Nobody expects tasting notes.

Tipping, in actual numbers

Tipping isn't required, but it's customary, especially if you don't end up buying a bottle. Rough guide for Napa specifically:

Stand-up bar tasting — $10–15 per couple
Seated tasting — $15–20 per couple
Boutique or private/VIP experience — $20 or more per couple

If you do buy wine and the host went out of their way — an off-script pour, extra time, a genuinely memorable visit — $10–20 per person on top is a generous way to say thanks. It's never expected.

The wine club pitch — totally fine to decline

Most tastings end with an invitation to join the winery's club. There's zero obligation. "I'll think about it" or a simple "not today, thanks" is a completely normal response, and it won't sour the visit or embarrass you. Only join if you'd genuinely want the shipments.

A few small practical things

By California law, only tasting-room staff can pour — reaching for an open bottle yourself isn't done. Bring ID; it's checked regularly, not as a judgment call. And if you can, visit on a weekday: a Saturday afternoon at a popular winery means a packed bar and quick pours, while a Tuesday morning at the same estate can mean twenty unhurried minutes with the person who actually made the wine.
