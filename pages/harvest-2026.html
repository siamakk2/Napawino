Harvest 2026: Early, Small, and Very Good

Napa Wino editors · Updated September 2026 · Editorially independent

The valley is working. Tractors on Highway 29 before sunrise, crush pads sticky, and the whole place smelling faintly of fermenting fruit. Harvest 2026 came early — early enough that some wineries are calling it the earliest on record — and the crop is light. Both are good news for the wine and useful news for anyone choosing when to visit.

What actually happened

The first grapes came off in July. Round Pond Estate picked sparkling base from its Rutherford vineyard on 21 July — six and a half tons, the traditional opening act, picked early to hold acidity. By 6 August the first Sauvignon Blanc had reached Napa Wine Co.'s Oakville crush pad, trucked down from Yountville. Inglenook reports the earliest start in its history.

The crop is smaller than usual, which in Napa almost always means better wine — less fruit per vine, more concentration in what's there. Round Pond's winemaker Jeff Plant called it a small but mighty crop and is unusually enthusiastic about the whites. Take that with the normal grain of harvest optimism, but the early reports are good.

Reported by the Napa Valley Register. The Napa Valley Vintners track the vintage week by week at harvestnapa.com.

The picking order

Harvest doesn't arrive all at once. Sparkling base comes first from Carneros, then still whites — Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay — then Pinot Noir, and Cabernet Sauvignon last. The sequence barely changes year to year; only the start date moves. In a normal year the whole thing runs eight to ten weeks. In 2026 it started in July, so the back half is running ahead of the calendar.

Practical translation: Cabernet is coming in during September this year rather than October. If you want to watch Napa pick the grape it's famous for, the window is now, not Columbus Day weekend.

Our call: come in September

Most guides tell you October. Most guides are working off an average year. This isn't one.

September gets you active crush pads, sorting tables running, and cellar crews who are busy but not yet destroyed. October in an early year gets you a quieter valley and the same tasting fee. The trade is crowds — September is busier and rooms cost more — but you're paying for the thing you came to see.

If your dates are fixed and they're late October, don't despair. The vineyards turn gold, the light is the best of the year, and the wineries have time to talk to you. A different trip, not a worse one.

Seeing it up close

Harvest is the one season when a tasting room visit can turn into something else entirely. Wineries open crush pads to guests, and a few will let you near the sorting line. The catch is that nobody can promise you a pick — fruit comes in when the numbers say so, often at 4am, and a winery that expected Tuesday might go Sunday.

So book the tasting for the wine and treat the harvest activity as a bonus. Ask at booking whether they expect to be crushing that week. The good ones will tell you honestly.

In Calistoga, Schramsberg's caves are the sparkling story and the fruit came in early. St. Helena and Rutherford put you closest to the Cabernet action. Yountville is the walkable base if you'd rather not drive during harvest traffic — and there is harvest traffic, mostly tractors, mostly at dawn.

One thing to watch

Forecasters are calling for a possible El Niño winter in Napa County, with above-average rainfall on the table. Past strong El Niño years have delivered mixed results, so this isn't a prediction so much as something to watch if you're booking spring. Wet winters make for green hills in March and occasionally for closed mountain roads.

Tell the sommelier which week you're coming and it'll build the day around what's actually being picked — crush pads in the morning, the quieter estates after.
