Napa Valley Harvest 2026: Early, Light & Very Good

Napa Wino editors · Updated 24 October 2026 · Editorially independent, no paid placements

Plan a Napa day →
Harvest status: complete

The 2026 Napa Valley harvest in brief: it started on July 21 — among the earliest starts ever recorded — and most of the valley floor was picked by the last week of September, about a month ahead of average. A warm March pushed the vines forward; rain and wind during bloom left loose, light clusters, so yields fell roughly 30–50%. What did come in has winemakers excited: small berries, deep color, firm tannin and unusually bright acidity. One grower's summary has become the vintage's shorthand: “quality up, yield down.”

The 2026 harvest, week by week

  • MarchAn unusually warm spring gives the valley about double its typical growing-degree days by month's end. Early-pruned vineyards push buds and green shoots straight into the heat.
  • April – MayCool, wet weather and wind arrive during bloom. Flowers fail to set — shatter — and the crop is cut before summer begins.
  • July 21Round Pond Estate's crew from Colinas Farming Company picks 6.5 tons of Chardonnay in Rutherford for the estate's sparkling Brut rosé. Mumm Napa and Paula Kornell schedule their first Pinot Noir picks for the same week — Kornell says that fruit is usually picked in the last week of August.
  • About July 24Chandon brings in 30 tons of Chardonnay before dawn — its earliest first pick in 53 years, beating the August 3, 2021 record. Inglenook picks Sauvignon Blanc at the end of July, the earliest start in its history.
  • July 31 – August 20Hyde Vineyards in Carneros and Antinori on Atlas Peak start picking on July 31; Antinori's Chardonnay is finished by August 20. Chateau Montelena starts Calistoga Chardonnay on August 2. St. Supéry has all its Rutherford Sauvignon Blanc in by August 15.
  • August 31Mueller Vineyard on Diamond Mountain picks Cabernet Sauvignon on the last day of August — a record, with the crop about 70% below average.
  • Early SeptemberCool, rainy days slow ripening, then three straight days above 100°F (September 8–10 in St. Helena) push sugar fast. Growers cool canopies with sprinklers; damage is limited.
  • September 10 – 30Cabernet season. Round Pond brings in its first Oakville Cabernet on September 10 and more than 10% of its annual fruit in a single day. Stags Leap District wraps up in the fourth week of September; Peju and Chateau Montelena finish on September 28 after an eight-week run.
  • October 2 – 16Hillside stragglers on Diamond Mountain, Atlas Peak and in Chiles Valley come off the vine. Heidi Peterson Barrett's last fruit arrives October 2; Kirk Venge finishes October 16 with Carneros Merlot and Oak Knoll Cabernet, his earliest finish on record.

Why it came early — and light

Two weather events, two months apart, made this vintage.

The early part was March. A run of warm weather arrived while many vineyards already had green tissue exposed. Those vines raced ahead — bud break, then bloom, weeks early. Vineyards pruned later largely dodged the surge, which is why 2026 ran at different speeds across the valley: some blocks came in during August while neighbors hung into October. As Spence Vineyards' winemaker Charlie Jensen put it, the biggest story was “how weird the pace of this harvest has been.”

The light part was bloom. Heavy rain in April and early May, plus cold and wind at the wrong moment, caused shatter — called coulure in Bordeaux — where flowers fail to become berries. Clusters came in loose, with gaps. Less fruit per vine then ripened faster, which fed back into the early timing. A mild summer with only brief heat spikes let the remaining fruit mature steadily; David Arthur Vineyards' Nile Zacherle described a mild summer and a smooth ripening period.

Spring Mountain's Stu Smith (Smith-Madrone) made the point that most North Coast growers farmed no differently this year — the weather did the thinning. Chateau Montelena's Matt Crafton: “Nature seems to have done the thinning for us this year.”

What winemakers are saying

Consulting winemaker Thomas Rivers Brown, who works with about 30 clients, went further: Napa may be on a run of four exceptional vintages in a row — 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026. Take early harvest praise with the usual grain of salt; the real verdict comes out of barrel in a year or two.

The business side: a small crop in a soft market

The light crop landed in an awkward market. Wine sales have slipped for several years and wineries are carrying inventory; in 2025 Napa crushed about 144,000 tons, down from about 170,000 in 2023, at an average of $6,767 a ton — still the highest price in California, but down 2.6%. Some growers went into 2026 without buyers for their fruit.

The short crop helped. In July, Napa Valley Grapegrowers' Caleb Mosley said wineries unsure of their yields were already phoning growers to buy fruit — a sign the slow grape market was starting to stir. By August, Calistoga growers who had waited all season for buyers were getting offers. But the relief was uneven: growers report that the smaller crop has not restored lost contracts, and one told Napa Valley Features that some Cabernet sold for as little as $1,000 a ton. The state's preliminary crush report, due around February 2027, will show whether 2026 prices held.

What it means for you as a buyer: less 2026 wine will exist. For small-production and cult labels, mailing lists and allocations will be tighter. For everyday Napa bottles, inventory from big earlier vintages means 2026 is unlikely to move shelf prices much soon.

Visiting Napa now: late October to winter

The fruit is in, but the cellars are not quiet. November is pressing, barreling down and the first tastes of new wine — and the valley floor turns gold and red as the vines shut down. Crowds thin, weekday tastings get easier to book, and hotel rates soften after the October peak. Mornings are cold and often foggy; afternoons can still be warm. Dress in layers.

Planning harvest 2027

When to come

In a typical year, late September to mid-October is peak crush, with Cabernet coming in across the valley. But 2026 was a reminder that the calendar moves: early years shift the action into August and September. Watch spring news — a warm March means an early harvest. Mid-to-late August is the safe bet for sparkling and white picks; mid-September for overlap of whites and reds.

How to actually see a crush

  • Book the tasting for the wine; treat the crush as a bonus. Fruit comes in when the numbers say so, often before dawn, and a pick planned for Tuesday can move to Sunday. Ask at booking whether they expect to be crushing that week.
  • Book harvest experiences by Labor Day. Grape stomps and harvest dinners are small and sell out.
  • Go early. Crews start at first light, and the valley is at its most atmospheric before 9 a.m.
  • Look for these annual harvest events, all of which ran in 2026 (confirm 2027 dates with the hosts): the Napa Valley Wine Train's Grape Stomp with Grgich Hills Estate (September–October), Peju's Harvest Festival & Grape Stomp, Clif Family's Farm Fest on Howell Mountain (the one day a year the farm opens), and Cakebread Cellars' Wines, Vines & Vinyl. The Napa Valley Vintners post the full list at napavalley.wine.

Where to stay

Up-valley towns — St. Helena and Calistoga — put you closest to the Cabernet action and book out first. Yountville is the walkable base if you'd rather not drive in harvest traffic. Napa has the widest range of rooms and prices. Compare them in Which Napa Valley town should you stay in?

Getting around

Harvest traffic is real — tractors and gondolas on Highway 29 at dawn, visitors at midday. Travel midweek if you can, use the Silverado Trail for north–south runs, and keep to two or three tastings a day. If you're tasting, don't drive: book a driver or rideshare.

Harvest 2026 FAQ

When did the 2026 Napa harvest start and finish?

It started on July 21, 2026, when Round Pond Estate in Rutherford picked Chardonnay for sparkling wine — one of only a handful of Napa harvests ever to start in July (2014 and 2015 were the others). Most valley-floor wineries finished in the last week of September; the last hillside Cabernet came in during the first half of October, with Venge Vineyards finishing on October 16.

Is 2026 a good vintage in Napa Valley?

Early signs are very good. Winemakers report small berries, concentrated color and tannin, and unusually bright acidity, and several compare it to 2021 and 2023. The crop is small — yields were commonly 30–50% below normal — and some expect variation from block to block because of the spring weather.

Why was the 2026 Napa harvest so early?

An unusually warm March gave Napa roughly double its typical growing-degree days by the end of the month, pushing bud break and bloom weeks ahead. Light crops ripen faster, and a mild summer with only brief heat spikes let the fruit mature steadily.

Why were yields so low?

Rain, cool temperatures and wind arrived during bloom in April and May, causing shatter (coulure): flowers failed to set, leaving loose clusters with fewer berries. Many blocks picked at about half their usual weight; some hillside sites far less.

When can I drink 2026 Napa wines?

Sauvignon Blanc and rosé typically release in spring and summer 2027; Chardonnay from late 2027; most Napa Cabernet Sauvignon spends 18–24 months in barrel and releases in 2028–2029.

When should I visit Napa to see the harvest?

In an average year, late August through mid-October, with Cabernet peaking late September into October. In early years like 2026 the action shifts to August and September. Book harvest experiences by Labor Day, keep your schedule flexible, and plan on early mornings.

Can I still see harvest activity in November?

Not picking — the 2026 fruit is all in. But wineries are pressing, barreling and tasting new wine, the vineyards are at their most colorful, and it's one of the easiest times of year to get a tasting appointment.
