Itineraries That Survive Contact With Highway 29

Napa Wino editors · Updated September 2026 · Editorially independent

Most Napa itineraries are wish lists. Five wineries, a long lunch, a spa, sunset somewhere photogenic. The valley is 30 miles long with one crowded road down the middle, and the wish list falls apart by two in the afternoon.

These are built backwards from the driving. Three tastings is the ceiling. Two is often better.

The one-day rules

Three tastings, maximum. Each is 60 to 90 minutes if it's any good. Add 15 to 25 minutes of driving between stops and ten to park and find the door. Three fills a day honestly. Four means rushing the one you came for.

Book the appointment-only place first. It's the fixed point. Everything else bends around it.

Go north to south, or south to north. Not both. Highway 29 and the Silverado Trail run parallel, and the roads connecting them are few. Zigzagging costs an hour you won't notice losing until it's gone.

Lunch between stops two and three, somewhere walk-in. A reservation you have to make by 1:15 turns the morning into a schedule.

One day, first visit

Start in Yountville. Most walkable town in the valley, highest density of good things, and you can do a morning without moving the car. One tasting in town, lunch on foot, then drive north for one more in Rutherford or St. Helena. Two tastings, no stress, and you'll have seen the place rather than the inside of three tasting rooms.

The version most people book — Napa for breakfast, Yountville for lunch, St. Helena for two tastings, Calistoga for sunset — is four towns in a day. Don't.

One day, been before

Go up. The valley floor is where everyone else is. Spring Mountain, Howell Mountain and Diamond Mountain put you above the traffic with better views and smaller crowds, and the wines are structurally different — firmer, more tannic, built to age.

The cost is driving. Mountain roads run slower than the map suggests and cell service disappears. Two stops is the limit, and you want the second to be the appointment you care about, because you'll arrive later than planned.

Two days, the good version

Day one — south. Downtown Napa in the morning, Carneros or Stags Leap in the afternoon. Sleep in Yountville or Napa.

Day two — north. Rutherford and St. Helena for Cabernet, then end in Calistoga with a mud bath. Booking the spa for late afternoon after the last tasting is the single best sequencing decision available in this valley.

Splitting the valley in half by day is the whole trick. You drive each stretch of 29 once instead of four times.

Two days, harvest

Different rules apply right now. Crush pads are active and some wineries will let you watch — but fruit comes in when the sugar says so, often before dawn, and nobody can promise a pick on a given day.

Book the tastings for the wine, not the spectacle, and ask at booking whether they expect to be crushing that week. Start earlier than you otherwise would: mornings are when the work happens, and tractors on 29 at 7am are slower than tourists on 29 at 11am. See the harvest guide for what's being picked now.

What we'd cut

The Napa Valley Wine Train is a good three hours if you want a long lunch and no driving. It is not a good way to see wineries — you see them through a window. Book it as a meal, not as a tasting day.

Sterling's aerial tram has been closed since the 2020 Glass Fire. Check its current status before putting it on a list; plenty of guides still include it as though nothing happened.

And the cult names — Screaming Eagle, Harlan, Promontory, Colgin — aren't visitable. Allocation list only. Any itinerary listing them is copying from another itinerary.

Tell the sommelier how many days you have and which town you're sleeping in, and it'll build the rest.
